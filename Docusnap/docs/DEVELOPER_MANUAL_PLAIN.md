# Scan Finder — How It Works, in Plain English

*A complete, non-technical tour of how the whole app works and how every part fits together.
No programming knowledge needed. Where a technical word is unavoidable, it's explained the first
time. For the code-level version, see `DEVELOPER_MANUAL.md`; for the always-current architecture
index, see `CLAUDE.md`.*

---

## 1. What Scan Finder is, and what it does

Scan Finder is a **Windows desktop program that files your scanned paperwork for you**. You point
it at a pile of scanned documents (invoices, orders, statements — anything), and it:

1. **Reads** each page (turning the picture of the paper into actual text),
2. **Understands** it (works out who it's from, what type it is, the date, the reference number, the total, and any other fields you care about),
3. **Files** it into a tidy folder structure with a sensible name, and
4. **Gets better every time you correct it** — so the more you use it, the less it needs you.

Two things make it unusual:

- **It runs entirely on your own PC.** Nothing is sent to the internet or a cloud service. Your
  documents never leave the machine. (There's an *optional* add-on that lets other PCs on your
  office network search the filed documents — more on that later — but even that stays inside your
  network.)
- **It learns your suppliers.** It isn't a fixed template you have to configure. It watches how you
  correct it and quietly builds up knowledge of each supplier's paperwork, so recurring documents
  eventually file themselves.

Think of it as a **very diligent filing clerk** who reads every document, files it exactly how you
like, remembers every correction you make, and never gets bored.

## 2. The journey of one document

This is the heart of the app. Here's what happens, step by step, from the moment a scan lands to the
moment it's filed.

**Step 1 — It arrives.** You either drop scans into a "watched" folder (the app notices new files
automatically) or you pick a folder and press *Process*. Either way, the very first thing the app
does is **make its own private copy** of each file. From that point on it works on the copy, so even
if the original is moved or deleted, nothing is lost. The original is set aside in a "Processed"
folder, untouched.

**Step 2 — It reads the page (OCR).** "OCR" (Optical Character Recognition) is the technology that
turns a *picture* of text into *actual, searchable text*. A scan is just a photo of paper — the
computer sees pixels, not words. OCR is the step that says "those pixels spell INVOICE." Scan Finder
uses a well-established open-source OCR engine called **Tesseract** for this.

Two clever touches here:

- If the document is a **"born-digital" PDF** (a PDF generated by a computer, not scanned from
  paper — e.g. an emailed invoice), it already contains real text under the hood, so the app reads
  that directly and skips the slow, error-prone OCR entirely. Faster and perfect.
- If a page was scanned **sideways or upside-down**, the app detects that and rotates it upright
  before reading, so a rotated scan doesn't turn into gibberish.

There's also a subtle but important trick: when OCR reads a page, it doesn't just dump the words out
in a jumble. It notes **where** each word sits on the page and rebuilds the text in proper reading
order. This matters because on an invoice, the word "Total:" might be on the left and the figure
"£387.74" far over on the right — a naïve reader treats those as two separate columns and loses the
connection. Scan Finder keeps them on the same line, so "Total" and its amount stay together.

**Step 3 — It works out what's on the page (extraction).** This is the "understanding" step, and
it's done as a series of increasingly clever attempts — a bit like a person glancing at a document
and picking out the important bits. In order:

- **"Do I recognise this layout?"** If you've filed documents from this supplier before, the app
  may already have a *template* for them — a remembered picture of where everything sits — and can
  jump straight to the right spots. It recognises the layout partly by the supplier's **logo**
  (it takes a kind of visual fingerprint of the logo) and partly by the distinctive words on the page.
- **"Where are the labels?"** It looks for labels like "Invoice No", "Date", "Total" and grabs the
  value next to each. It's tolerant of OCR quirks (it'll still find "Invoice No" even if OCR ran the
  words together as "InvoiceNo").
- **"Where did I find this last time?"** If you've previously *pointed out* where a field lives on
  this supplier's documents (by drawing a box around it — see §3), the app goes straight to that spot
  and reads it, even re-reading that little region of the image for accuracy.
- **"Does anything need tidying?"** It cleans up common OCR slip-ups it has learned for this supplier
  (for example, an "O" that should be a zero), and fills in stable fields it's confident about from
  past experience.
- **"Does it all add up?"** Finally it sanity-checks: does the subtotal plus VAT roughly equal the
  total? Is the date a real, sensible date (not 40 years in the future)? Does each field look like the
  *shape* this supplier's values usually take? If something's off, it doesn't silently accept it — it
  lowers its confidence and flags the field for you to check.

**Step 4 — It scores its own confidence.** For every field, and for the document overall, the app
gives itself a confidence score out of 100. High confidence means "I'm sure." Low confidence, a
failed sanity check, or a missing required field means "a human should look at this."

**Step 5 — You check it (Review).** Documents that need a look land in the **Review** queue. You see
the scan on one side and the extracted fields on the other, each with its confidence and any warning
notes. You fix anything wrong, then confirm. (There are keyboard shortcuts to make this fast —
**Ctrl+Enter** files the document, **Space** marks it reviewed, **↑/↓** move between documents.)

**Every correction you make is a lesson.** When you fix "Acme Ltd" to "Acme Limited," or move the
reference-number box to the right place, the app remembers — scoped to that supplier and document
type — and applies it next time.

**Step 6 — It's filed.** On confirm, the document is copied to its final home. By default that's
`Company / Year / Month / DocumentType.Date.Reference.pdf` (all of which you can customise with a
simple click-together pattern builder). A small companion file recording the extracted details is
saved alongside it. If a file with the same name already exists, it adds a "-DUPLICATE" suffix rather
than overwriting.

**Step 7 (sometimes) — it files itself.** Once the app is *very* sure about a document — everything
filled in, nothing flagged, top confidence — it can file it **automatically**, without you touching
Review at all. This is deliberately cautious (see §4), and it's how a well-trained system eventually
handles routine paperwork on its own.

## 3. How it gets smarter

Scan Finder has, in effect, several **memories**, all built up quietly from the documents you confirm.
None of them are pre-programmed for your suppliers — they grow from your corrections.

- **Logo memory.** It remembers each supplier's logo as a compact visual fingerprint, so it can
  recognise "this is from Acme" on sight next time — even if the new scan is a slightly different size
  or quality.
- **Label-position memory ("anchors").** When you draw a box around a field's value to teach it (the
  ⊕ tool), it remembers *where that field sits relative to its label* on this supplier's layout — and,
  crucially, it remembers the label→value *relationship*, so it still works if the next scan is shifted
  a bit on the page.
- **Templates.** For suppliers you see often, it builds a fuller remembered layout, and an admin can
  even draw permanent "read this field from here" mappings. If a supplier tweaks their layout slightly,
  the app can re-align to the change rather than getting confused.
- **Correction memory.** Every value you correct is stored (scoped to that supplier + document type +
  field). This feeds two things: learned fixes for common OCR misreads, and a learned sense of what a
  field's values normally *look like* (so an out-of-shape value gets flagged).
- **Graduation.** After a supplier's documents have been confirmed cleanly enough times, that
  supplier "graduates" — the app trusts it enough to start auto-filing its routine documents.

A key principle runs through all of this: **learning is always scoped narrowly.** A lesson learned
for one supplier's invoices never leaks onto a different supplier or a different document type. This
is what stops the app from "helpfully" stamping the wrong company's details onto an unrelated document.

## 4. The safety checks — why it rarely files the wrong thing

Filing paperwork wrong is worse than not filing it, so the app is built to be **cautious by default**.
The safeguards:

- **Confidence gating.** Anything the app isn't sure about goes to Review, not straight to file.
- **Sanity checks.** Totals must roughly add up; dates must be plausible; values must match the shape
  the field usually takes. A failure doesn't get silently accepted — it lowers confidence and flags
  the field.
- **The supplier-letterhead cross-check (new).** A common, nasty mistake in this kind of software is
  filing a document under the *wrong company* — for instance, reading the "Bill To" customer as if it
  were the supplier, or matching the wrong logo. Scan Finder now independently reads the **company name
  printed at the top of the page** (the letterhead) and compares it to whichever supplier the rest of
  the process decided on. If they disagree, it doesn't guess — it sends the document to Review with a
  clear note: *"Letterhead may read X — detected Y. Please confirm."* It never silently overrides your
  data; it only raises a hand. This was measured on real filed documents at 99.4% accuracy with zero
  false alarms before being switched on by default.
- **Auto-file only when certain.** Automatic filing only happens for fully-filled, unflagged, top-
  confidence documents — or for suppliers that have earned trust through a run of clean confirmations.
  The moment anything is uncertain, the document waits for you.
- **Nothing is destroyed.** Originals are kept, duplicates are suffixed rather than overwritten, and a
  document sent back for re-checking replaces its filed copy in place rather than creating a mess.

The guiding rule: **a missed catch (sending a fine document to Review unnecessarily) is a minor
annoyance; a silent mis-file is a real problem — so the app always errs toward asking.**

## 5. How the pieces fit together

It helps to picture Scan Finder as **three cooperating parts**:

1. **The window you see (the "front of house").** This is the desktop app itself — the dashboard,
   the Review screen, Settings, Search, the teaching wizards. It's what you click on. Technically it's
   built with a toolkit called **Electron** (the same technology behind many desktop apps), which is
   essentially a dedicated, locked-down web page running as a Windows program.

2. **The reader (the "back of house").** The actual reading-and-understanding work — OCR and field
   extraction — is done by a set of **Python** programs that run quietly in the background whenever
   documents are processed. The front-of-house app hands them a batch of files and listens for the
   results streaming back (which document, what fields, how confident). You never see this directly;
   it's the engine room.

3. **The filing cabinet and the notebook (storage).** Two kinds of storage:
   - Your **actual filed documents** live as normal PDF files in the folder structure on disk — you
     can open them in Explorer like any other file; the app isn't a black box holding them hostage.
   - A small **database** (a single file on your PC) is the app's *notebook*: it records everything it
     has learned (logos, label positions, templates, corrections), the list of documents and their
     status, your settings, users, and an audit log. This is what makes the learning persistent.

These parts talk through a deliberately narrow, secure channel, so the front-of-house window can't do
anything dangerous on its own — it can only *ask* the back-of-house to do specific, permitted jobs.

**Roles.** The app supports different **user roles** — an *admin* (full control, settings, teaching),
an *edit* user (process and confirm documents), and a *read-only* user (search and view only). What
each person can see and do is controlled both in the screens they're shown and, more importantly,
enforced behind the scenes so a read-only user genuinely cannot change anything.

**Processing modes.** There's a "Fast" and a "Smart" setting. Historically these differed, but today
they do the same thorough keyword-and-position read (an older experimental AI mode was removed
entirely, so there's no AI model, no internet call, nothing to install). Both are quick.

## 6. The optional LAN Search Client

The core app is complete on its own. But offices often want *other people* to be able to **search and
view** the filed documents without installing or touching the main app. For that there's a separate,
smaller companion program — the **Search Client** — that runs on other PCs on the same office network.

- It connects to the main PC over a **secure, encrypted link** that stays entirely inside your
  network (it uses the same kind of certificate-based security as banking websites, but private to you).
- Setting it up involves a one-time **pairing** step so only trusted PCs can connect.
- Depending on your licence, it offers **search and preview**, and optionally a **review/approval
  mailbox** (route a document to a colleague to approve) — though the approval feature is currently
  held back pre-release.
- It's an **add-on**: the core app works fully without it, and it's licensed separately.

## 7. Licensing and the trial

Scan Finder is commercial software with a **free trial** and then a **paid licence** tied to the
specific PC it's installed on.

- New installs get a **14-day trial**. After that, a licence key unlocks continued use.
- The licence is **device-bound** — checked against a fingerprint of the machine — so a key can't be
  copied around freely.
- Licence checks are designed to work **offline**: the app can verify a validly-signed licence without
  phoning home every time, using cryptographic signatures (a tamper-proof "seal" it can check locally).
- There's a separate small licensing server (run by the vendor) that issues and validates keys; the
  app talks to it only when activating or occasionally re-checking, never to process your documents.
- On first run, and after major updates, you're asked to accept the terms once; that acceptance is
  recorded locally.

The **seller of record** is a payment provider (Polar), and the product is published under the trading
name **Six Mile Software** — that's what appears on the website, emails, and legal pages.

## 8. Where your files and data live

Everything is on your own machine:

- **Your filed documents:** in the output folder *you* choose, organised as
  `Company / Year / Month / …`. Ordinary files you can back up or open however you like.
- **The app's private working copy** of each imported document: in the app's data area, so processing
  never depends on the original file surviving.
- **The learning + records database:** a single database file in the app's data area
  (`%APPDATA%\ScanFinder`). This holds the learned knowledge, document list, settings, users, and audit
  log. Deleting it resets the app to a blank slate (which you'd only ever do deliberately).
- **Nothing in the cloud.** There is no external storage, no telemetry of document contents, no
  document data leaving the PC.

Because your filed documents are just normal files and the "brain" is one database file, backing up is
simple and the app never traps your data.

## 9. When things go wrong

The app is built to **fail safely and visibly** rather than silently:

- **A document it can't read or is unsure about** → goes to the Review queue with the specific fields
  flagged and a note explaining why. Nothing is force-filed.
- **A page that jams the reader** (a corrupt or pathological scan) → the app has a watchdog that gives
  up on that one page after a timeout, marks it as an error, and moves on, so one bad document can't
  freeze the whole batch. The bad file is set aside for you to look at.
- **A mistaken lesson** (the app learned something wrong from a bad confirmation) → there are admin
  tools to view the learned values for a supplier/field, correct or remove a bad one, and "un-poison"
  a document type by sending a suspect filed document back to Review.
- **A wrong auto-file or a layout that drifted** → reprocessing a document re-reads it with the latest
  knowledge; a slightly-changed supplier layout can be re-aligned or re-taught.
- **First launch feels slow** → on a brand-new install, Windows security scans the unfamiliar program
  the first time it runs, which can make the very first launch take a minute; subsequent launches are
  normal.

The overall philosophy, end to end: **read everything, be honest about uncertainty, never destroy an
original, keep every correction as a lesson, and only act automatically when genuinely sure.**
